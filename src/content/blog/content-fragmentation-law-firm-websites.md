# Content Fragmentation: Why Your Law Firm Has 50 Pages on the Same Topic (And Fixes)

**Content fragmentation** is what happens when your website has multiple pages targeting the same keyword, topic, or searcher intent — and none of them rank well because they're competing with each other instead of with your actual competitors. Yes, it hurts law firm SEO. Badly. Google sees five versions of your "car accident attorney Tampa" page and has no clear signal about which one to rank. So it picks none of them, or it rotates them unpredictably, and your competitor with one authoritative page sits above you while you wonder why your traffic is down.

Should law firms consolidate similar pages? Usually, yes. But not always, and the wrong consolidation can make things worse. The fix depends on what you're actually dealing with — and most firms don't know what they're dealing with until they do the audit.

Here's how to map it, fix it, and avoid rebuilding the same problem in six months.

---

## What Content Fragmentation Actually Looks Like

Law firm websites accumulate fragmented content the same way filing cabinets accumulate paper: gradually, with good intentions, until nothing is findable.

Common patterns:

- A blog post from 2019 called "What to Do After a Car Accident"
- A practice area page called "Car Accident Lawyer"
- A landing page built for a Google Ads campaign: "Tampa Car Accident Attorney"
- A FAQ page that answers "What does a car accident lawyer do?"
- A "resources" page linking to all of the above
- A location page for each county your firm serves, all with near-identical copy

Each of these was created for a reason. Taken individually, none of them seem redundant. Collectively, they are a fragmentation disaster. Google is crawling all five, trying to decide which one matters most, and frequently concluding: none of them, really.

This is **content fragmentation SEO** in action at law firms — and it's more common than most managing partners realize.

## How to Audit Your Website for Overlapping Content

You don't need an enterprise tool to find fragmentation. You need a spreadsheet and about two hours of honest work.

**Step 1: Export every URL on your site.**
Use Screaming Frog (free up to 500 URLs), Sitebulb, or even your sitemap. List every page.

**Step 2: Add the target keyword for each page.**
If you don't know what keyword a page is targeting, that's already a problem — but write down what the page *seems* to be about.

**Step 3: Group by topic.**
Sort the list by topic cluster. Every page about car accidents in one group. Every page about divorce in another. When you see three or more URLs in the same cluster, you likely have fragmentation.

**Step 4: Pull ranking data.**
Use Google Search Console. For each URL, check which queries it's appearing for and what position it holds. If two pages in the same cluster are ranking for nearly identical queries, they are splitting your authority.

**Step 5: Check organic traffic per page.**
If you have five pages in a cluster and four of them get fewer than 10 organic sessions a month, those four are almost certainly diluting the one that matters.

This process surfaces the fragmentation problem clearly. What you do next depends on what you find.

## What to Consolidate and What to Keep Separate

Not everything that looks like fragmentation is actually a problem. Here's how to make the call.

**Consolidate when:**
- Multiple pages target the same primary keyword and searcher intent
- The pages have thin content that could be merged into one authoritative piece
- One page clearly outperforms the others and could absorb their backlinks and traffic via redirect
- The pages exist because different people created content over the years without a content map

**Keep separate when:**
- The pages serve genuinely different searcher intents (someone searching "car accident settlement amounts" wants different information than someone searching "car accident lawyer near me")
- Location pages target distinct geographic markets with meaningfully different content — not just the city name swapped out
- One page is informational (blog post explaining the law) and one is transactional (practice area page with a clear CTA)

The distinction matters because [keyword intent mismatch](/blog/keyword-intent-mismatch-law-firms/) is just as damaging as fragmentation. Merging a high-intent landing page with an informational blog post can destroy the conversion performance of the stronger asset.

If your pages serve the same intent, merge them. If they serve different intents, keep them separate but make sure they're clearly differentiated in both content and meta data.

---

## The Canonical and Redirect Mechanics

Once you've decided what to consolidate:

**Option 1: 301 redirect** the weaker pages to the primary page. This is the right call when the weaker pages have no meaningful unique content worth preserving. The redirect passes link equity to the primary page and removes the competing URL from Google's index.

**Option 2: Canonical tag** if you need to keep a page live for a non-SEO reason (like a paid landing page) but don't want it competing in organic search. The canonical tells Google which version is authoritative.

**Option 3: Merge and rewrite** when both pages have content worth keeping. Pull the best elements from each, write a stronger combined page, redirect the weaker URL to the new one, and update internal links site-wide.

Internal links matter more than most firms acknowledge. After consolidation, update every internal link pointing to the old URLs. If your navigation, blog posts, and resource pages still link to the redirected pages, you're leaving signal on the table.

This is also a good moment to reconsider whether your firm's content strategy has a [boutique specialization advantage](/blog/boutique-law-firm-seo-advantage/) to leverage — firms with tightly focused practice areas typically have less fragmentation because their content scope is narrower.

## Why Fragmentation Keeps Happening (And How to Stop It)

Fixing fragmentation once doesn't mean you've fixed the process that created it.

Most law firm content fragmentation comes from:

- **No editorial calendar or content map** — different people create content without checking what already exists
- **Vendor churn** — each new SEO vendor or content agency adds pages without auditing what's there
- **Campaign-driven content** — PPC landing pages get built and forgotten, then indexed and left to compete with organic pages
- **Blog content that overlaps practice area pages** — attorneys or marketing staff write helpful blog posts that accidentally target the same keywords as core service pages

The fix is a content governance process, not just a one-time cleanup. Before any new page goes live, someone needs to check: does this page already exist in some form? What keyword is it targeting, and do we already have a page targeting that keyword?

This also connects to a broader pattern worth examining. Firms that struggle with fragmentation often have the same underlying problem driving other marketing inefficiencies — no clear picture of what's actually working. If you're not sure whether your content is generating the right cases, the issue may run deeper than page count. [Lead quality versus lead volume](/blog/lead-quality-vs-volume-law-firms/) is often where that realization hits hardest.

Similarly, if you're spending on paid search alongside fragmented organic content, you're likely paying more per click than you should be. Fragmented sites often have worse [landing page performance](/blog/law-firm-landing-page-speed-conversions/) and lower quality scores, which drives up your cost per acquisition in ways that compound over time.

## The Consolidation Payoff

Done correctly, content consolidation almost always produces measurable ranking improvements within 60–90 days. You're concentrating authority that was previously split across five weak pages into one strong one. Google gets a clearer signal. The consolidated page starts accumulating more clicks, which reinforces the ranking signal further.

The firms that skip this work — or that keep adding new pages without addressing existing fragmentation — are essentially adding weight to a car with a flat tire and wondering why it won't go faster.
