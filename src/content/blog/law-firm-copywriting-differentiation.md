# The Law Firm Copywriting Problem: Why Your Service Pages Sound Like Every Other Firm

Law firms should write service pages differently than they currently do — which is to say, they should write them like actual businesses trying to win specific clients, not like brochures assembled by a committee afraid of saying anything concrete. The most common copywriting mistake law firms make is describing what they do instead of why it matters to the person reading it. The result is a page that sounds professional, hits all the practice area keywords, and tells the prospect absolutely nothing useful about whether your firm is the right choice for their situation.

**Law firm marketing copywriting** is not about sounding authoritative. It is about making a stranger feel like they found the right place. Those are different goals, and most firms are optimizing for the wrong one.

---

## What "Indistinguishable" Actually Looks Like

Open ten law firm websites in your practice area. Read their homepage and primary service page. Count how many use phrases like:

- "Experienced attorneys dedicated to protecting your rights"
- "Personalized attention for every client"
- "Aggressive representation with compassionate service"
- "Results-driven legal counsel"

All of them. Every single one. These phrases are not wrong — they are meaningless. They communicate nothing differentiating because every firm claims them. When everyone says the same thing, no one is saying anything.

The prospect who lands on your page has almost certainly already visited two or three competitors. If your copy reads the same way theirs does, you have not given them a reason to stay, call, or trust you. You have given them confirmation that lawyers all sound alike.

This is a **conversion problem** dressed up as a branding problem. And it is entirely fixable.

---

## Why This Keeps Happening

Law firm websites get written by one of three parties: an associate who was handed the task and wrote what felt safe, a marketing agency that used a legal template with swapped-in firm names, or a managing partner who approved copy based on how it felt rather than how it performs.

None of these processes start with the question that actually matters: *What does a prospect need to believe before they'll hire us, and are we saying it anywhere on this page?*

There is also a real fear of specificity in legal marketing. The more specific you are, the more you might exclude someone. But here is the practical reality: **vague copy excludes everyone** because it compels no one. A personal injury firm that says "we handle all types of injury cases" is less compelling than one that explains exactly how they handle insurance company negotiations and why their process produces different outcomes than a firm that settles fast to collect fees.

Specificity builds trust. Generality signals that you haven't thought carefully about your clients.

---

## How to Rewrite Copy That Actually Differentiates You

### Start With What Your Best Clients Already Know

The most useful raw material for differentiated copy is already sitting in your firm. It's in the intake conversations. It's in the questions clients ask on the first call. It's in the reasons past clients refer new ones.

If you ask your best clients what made them choose your firm or stay with it, they will not say "experienced attorneys" or "personalized attention." They will say something specific. That specific thing is what your service page copy should be built around.

This is not a marketing trick. It is listening, then writing down what you heard.

### Replace Category Claims With Mechanism

A **category claim** says what you are: experienced, aggressive, client-focused. A **mechanism** explains how you operate and why that matters. Mechanisms are differentiating. Category claims are not.

Bad: *"We provide aggressive representation to protect your interests."*

Better: *"We don't recommend settlement until we've run a full damages analysis — including future costs most firms don't calculate. That number changes the negotiation."*

The second version tells the prospect something they can evaluate. It implies a process. It creates a question in their mind: does my current attorney do that? That is the beginning of a real sales conversation.

This connects directly to how you structure the page. If you want help thinking through which elements actually move prospects to contact you, [conversion rate testing for law firm websites](/blog/law-firm-cro-testing-methodology/) is a more rigorous process than most firms run — and it starts with identifying what the copy is actually communicating versus what you think it communicates.

### Ditch the Generic Bio Section

Attorney bios are the worst offenders in law firm marketing copywriting. They list credentials, schools, bar admissions, and maybe a hobby or two. They read like a LinkedIn profile written in the third person by someone who finds the subject mildly embarrassing.

What prospects actually want from a bio is: *Can I trust this person? Do they understand situations like mine? Will they be straight with me?*

A bio that answers those questions is specific about the types of matters the attorney handles, what they actually do differently, and what it's like to work with them. That's a harder page to write. It's also a page that converts.

### Address the Real Objection on the Page

Every practice area has a primary objection that prospects don't say out loud but are thinking when they arrive. For personal injury, it might be: *Will this take forever and will I actually see any money?* For employment law: *Will this blow up my career?* For business litigation: *Is this going to cost more than it's worth?*

If your service page does not acknowledge and address that objection, you are leaving the prospect to answer it alone — usually by closing the tab.

Good **law firm marketing copywriting** names the fear, legitimizes it, and then explains your specific answer to it. That is what "addressing the reader" actually means. Not platitudes about dedication. An honest engagement with what they're worried about.

## The SEO Problem With Generic Copy

This is not just a conversion problem. Generic copy is also an SEO problem. Search engines have gotten better at identifying pages that are thin on substance — pages that use the right keywords but don't actually say anything worth reading.

If your service pages are built around broad terms and undifferentiated content, you may already be seeing the effects. [The Google Helpful Content Update has specific implications for law firm websites](/blog/google-helpful-content-update-law-firms/), and undifferentiated service pages are exactly what it was designed to deprioritize.

The fix is not keyword stuffing or adding more words. It's writing copy that actually reflects genuine expertise and specific perspective — which, conveniently, is also what converts prospects.

Niche practice areas have it particularly hard here. If you're in a specialized space, [content strategy for niche law practices](/blog/niche-law-practice-content-strategy/) requires a tighter focus on demonstrating actual subject matter depth, not just coverage of the right topics.

## What Differentiated Copy Requires

It requires someone willing to have an honest conversation about what the firm actually does well and who it actually serves best. That conversation is uncomfortable for some managing partners because it implies turning away work. But a firm that is clearly excellent for a defined type of client will outperform a firm that claims to be good for everyone, every time.

It also requires that the copy be reviewed for performance, not just appearance. Whether the page converts is a measurable outcome. Whether the copy "sounds like us" is a feeling. Make decisions based on the former.

If you're not sure where your copy is losing people — whether it's the page itself, the intake process, or what happens after a prospect fills out a form — [firms lose leads at multiple points after initial contact](/blog/law-firm-lead-follow-up-conversion/), and it's worth auditing the whole path before assuming the copy is the only problem.

---

## The Short Version

Your service pages sound like every other firm's because they were built to avoid saying anything wrong rather than to say something true and specific. The fix is not a design refresh. It is copy that names your actual clients, describes your actual process, and addresses the actual objection your prospects walk in with.

That is harder to write. It is significantly easier to convert.
