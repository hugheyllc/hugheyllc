# The Content Decay Problem: How Your Old Blog Posts Are Actively Hurting Your Rankings

Old blog posts hurt your rankings when Google determines the information is stale and users signal the same thing by bouncing. That's not a theory — it's how search quality evaluation works. If you published a post in 2020 about PPP loan forgiveness, SBA deadline extensions, or eviction moratoriums, and it's still sitting on your site without updates, you have a **outdated legal content SEO rankings** problem. Not a minor housekeeping issue. An active drag on your authority. The answer to whether you should delete or update outdated content is: almost always update, occasionally redirect, and rarely delete — but you need a framework to know which is which. That framework is what this post is about.

---

## Why Content Decays Faster in Law Than in Most Industries

Law is unusually vulnerable to content decay because **the underlying facts change constantly**. Statutes get amended. Agencies issue new guidance. Courts overturn precedents. Deadlines expire. Programs end.

When a food blogger's 2019 recipe post goes stale, nothing much happens. When your 2021 post about federal eviction moratoriums still ranks for searches that people are conducting today, something actively bad happens:

- Users land on outdated information
- They leave immediately (high bounce signal)
- Google registers that your page didn't satisfy the query
- Your domain takes a credibility hit on that topic cluster

This is **outdated legal content SEO rankings** damage in real time. You're not just failing to help a prospective client — you're burning authority you could be using elsewhere.

---

## The Three Categories of Decayed Content

Not all old posts are equally damaged. Before you start deleting things, categorize what you have.

**Category 1: Fully Expired Content**
This is the PPP loan post. The program is over. The deadline passed years ago. No one needs this information to make a legal decision today. It may still get organic traffic because the keywords appear related to current SBA or business loan questions — but that traffic converts at zero. These posts are either redirect candidates (if the topic connects to a current service) or deletion candidates (if it's truly a dead end with minimal link equity).

**Category 2: Partially Outdated Content**
This is more common and more dangerous, because it looks fine from the outside. A 2019 post about employment discrimination procedures, for example, may have accurate general information but reference outdated EEOC filing deadlines, old case law, or pre-pandemic remote work assumptions. It's not wrong enough to obviously fail users, but it's wrong enough to undermine your credibility with anyone who knows better — including Google's quality evaluators.

**Category 3: Evergreen Content With Stale Details**
These are your best candidates for recovery. The topic is permanently relevant — statute of limitations for personal injury, how to form an LLC, what to do after a DUI — but the post hasn't been touched since it was published. The core is sound; the execution is dated. These posts should be updated on a **12-to-18-month review cycle**, minimum. High-traffic evergreen posts deserve a 6-month look.

## The Surgery: What to Actually Do With Decayed Posts

### Step 1: Pull the Data First

Don't guess. Go into Google Search Console and sort your posts by impressions and clicks over the last 12 months. Look for posts that are generating impressions but have low click-through rates, or posts that get clicks but have high bounce rates in Analytics. Those are your decay signals.

Also flag anything that:
- References specific deadlines that have passed
- Cites laws or regulations that have since changed
- Was written in response to a temporary event (COVID relief, specific legislation, a particular court ruling)

### Step 2: Update Before You Delete

The default move should be updating. Deleting a page eliminates whatever link equity it has accumulated, removes it from any internal linking structure you've built, and throws away whatever indexation history exists. That's expensive.

When you update, do it properly. **Don't just change the date.** Google is not fooled by a timestamp refresh on content that hasn't substantively changed. Rewrite the sections that are factually outdated. Add new context. Update your calls to action. If there's a related current service page, link to it — and make sure the service page is properly positioned in your [site hierarchy](/blog/law-firm-service-page-hierarchy/) so that link passes meaningful weight.

### Step 3: Consolidate When You Have Duplicates

Content decay often reveals a related problem: you've written about the same topic multiple times over the years, and now you have three posts competing for the same query. This is the [practice area cannibalization problem](/blog/practice-area-cannibalization-seo/) at the content level. Pick the strongest post, merge the others into it, and redirect the old URLs. One authoritative page beats three diluted ones every time.

### Step 4: Redirect or Delete as a Last Resort

If a post covers a topic that no longer connects to any service you offer, and it has no meaningful link equity, and it generates zero useful traffic — delete it and let the URL return a 404, then eventually remove it from your sitemap. If it has some link equity but the topic is dead, redirect it to your most relevant practice area page or your blog index.

Don't redirect everything to your homepage. That's a lazy redirect and Google knows it.

## The Frequency Question

Managing partners often ask how often they should be updating old content. The honest answer is that most firms should be doing a **content audit annually** and updating high-traffic evergreen posts at least once a year. Practice areas that touch frequently changing law — immigration, tax, employment, regulatory compliance — may need a 6-month cycle on key posts.

This is not as burdensome as it sounds if you're not also running on the content treadmill of publishing new posts every week. If you've been operating under the assumption that more content is always better, it's worth reading about [a law firm SEO strategy that doesn't require constant content production](/blog/law-firm-seo-without-content-treadmill/). Maintaining what you have is often more valuable than adding to the pile.

The firms that have good content hygiene also tend to be better at [content repurposing](/blog/content-repurposing-strategy-law-firms/) — which means updated posts become refreshed social content, newsletter material, or talking points for video. You're not starting from scratch every time.

## What This Has to Do With Your Overall Authority

Here's the part that gets underappreciated: **outdated legal content SEO rankings** problems are not just about individual posts. When Google crawls your site and finds a pattern of stale, low-engagement content, it affects how the entire domain is evaluated for freshness and reliability signals. You can have excellent cornerstone service pages and still be dragged down by a graveyard of old blog posts nobody is maintaining.

This is also why content decay audits belong in a broader [law firm marketing audit](/blog/law-firm-marketing-audit-revenue-prediction/) — not treated as a separate SEO task. Your content quality affects your rankings, your rankings affect your traffic, your traffic affects your intake volume, and your intake volume is revenue. The chain is direct. Ignoring content decay at one end eventually shows up as a pipeline problem at the other.

If you've noticed your organic traffic plateauing or declining and you're not sure why, there's a good chance decayed content is part of the diagnosis — along with the other factors covered in [why your law firm's SEO stalled](/blog/law-firm-seo-plateau-diagnosis/).

## The Bottom Line

That 2020 blog post is not neutral. It is either helping you or hurting you.
