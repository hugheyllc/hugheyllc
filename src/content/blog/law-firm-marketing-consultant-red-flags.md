# The Law Firm Marketing Consultant Red Flag Checklist

Hiring a **law firm marketing consultant** is straightforward until you've done it once and gotten burned. Here's what the red flags actually look like: a consultant who leads with deliverables instead of diagnosis, someone who can't name a single thing they'd change about your current site without seeing it first, and anyone whose proposal arrives within 24 hours of your first call. Speed is not competence. A consultant who hasn't asked enough questions to slow down doesn't understand your practice—they're recycling a template.

Evaluating consultants isn't about checking credentials. It's about pressure-testing how they think. The checklist below is what partners who've been through a few bad engagements actually use before signing. It won't make the decision for you, but it will eliminate the wrong candidates fast.

---

## Red Flag #1: They Lead With Tactics, Not Diagnosis

The first thing any competent **law firm marketing consultant** should want to understand is where your current marketing is broken. Not what shiny thing they can build you—where the existing machine is leaking.

If the opening conversation is dominated by what the consultant *offers* rather than what your firm *needs*, walk away. The right consultant asks about your intake process, your current lead sources, which practice areas you're trying to grow, and what's already been tried. They don't start pitching until they've listened enough to know whether their pitch even makes sense for you.

This connects directly to a deeper problem: firms don't often know where their marketing is quietly failing them. A consultant who doesn't dig into [why your website is leaking leads through broken qualification](/blog/law-firm-lead-qualification-leaks/) before proposing a solution is guessing. And you're paying for the guess.

---

## Red Flag #2: Generic Case Studies, Vague Results

Every consultant has case studies. Almost none of them hold up under a follow-up question.

"We increased traffic by 300%" — from what baseline? Over what time period? In what practice area? Did that traffic convert into consultations, or did you just attract a lot of people searching for things you don't actually handle?

Results without context are marketing, not evidence. Push on specifics. Ask what the firm was struggling with before the engagement, what strategy was implemented, how long it took to show results, and what the measurement methodology was. If the answers get vague, that tells you something.

A consultant who has genuinely done this work can explain the difference between a [keyword gap that creates opportunity versus one that just looks good in a report](/blog/keyword-gap-analysis-law-firms/). If they can't, they're probably reporting on activity rather than outcomes.

---

## Red Flag #3: They Can't Tell You What They Won't Do

This one is counterintuitive. You'd think the more a consultant offers, the better. The opposite is true.

Full-service marketing agencies that handle everything from your parking lot signage to your Google Ads campaign are, by definition, mediocre at most of it. Specialization matters in legal marketing because the nuances are real—local search for a multi-office firm has [specific consolidation traps](/blog/multi-office-law-firm-geo-keywords/) that a generalist consultant will walk you straight into. The website copy that sounds like a law firm instead of a chatbot requires a [different approach than standard conversion copywriting](/blog/law-firm-website-copy-authentic-voice/).

Ask any consultant you're evaluating: "What do you *not* do, and who would you refer us to for that?" A confident, competent answer earns trust. Hesitation or a pivot back to their full-service capabilities is a red flag.

---

## Red Flag #4: No Opinion on Your Current Website

Before any engagement, you should send a prospective consultant your website and ask for a candid read. Not a formal audit—just an honest first impression.

If they come back with generic praise followed by a soft suggestion that "there's always room to improve," they're not being straight with you. A consultant who has looked at hundreds of law firm websites will have an immediate, specific reaction. They'll notice if your homepage buries the practice areas, if your attorney bios read like CVs, if [your wireframe is built for your designer's portfolio rather than client conversion](/blog/law-firm-website-conversion-wireframe/), or if you have a pile of underperforming pages that are quietly diluting your domain authority.

Opinions are a feature, not a liability. You're not hiring a consultant to validate your current decisions. If they won't tell you what's wrong when there's nothing on the line, they won't tell you when there is.

---

## Red Flag #5: They Don't Ask About Your Competitors

Legal marketing doesn't happen in a vacuum. If a consultant doesn't ask who your primary competitors are, what firms are ranking where you want to rank, and what those firms are doing differently—they're operating blind.

Competitive context changes strategy significantly. What works in a market with two dominant firms looks different from a market with fifteen equally matched ones. A consultant who skips this conversation is probably applying the same playbook to every client regardless of competitive landscape.

The same logic applies to their knowledge of local search dynamics. [How the local pack algorithm is shifting](/blog/google-local-pack-algorithm-changes/) affects a firm in a different way depending on its current position, its review velocity, and its citation profile. A consultant who can't walk you through that at a high level before engagement starts is going to learn on your dime.

---

## Red Flag #6: They Can't Explain SEO Trade-offs

Legal SEO is full of trade-offs that require judgment calls. If a consultant presents every decision as obvious and without downside, they're oversimplifying.

For example: aggressive paid ad strategies can [affect your organic rankings in ways that aren't always disclosed](/blog/google-guaranteed-legal-ads-seo-impact/). Niche specialization sounds smart on paper but [can actually hurt rankings compared to a broader practice mix](/blog/niche-law-firm-seo-specialization-trap/). Producing a lot of content fast creates [dead weight pages that drag down the whole domain](/blog/law-firm-content-audit-delete-strategy/) if the quality isn't there.

A consultant who understands the trade-offs will explain them clearly and tell you which direction they'd go and why. One who doesn't will give you the confident answer that sells the engagement rather than the honest one that serves the firm.

---

## Questions to Ask Before You Sign

If you want to run a tighter evaluation, here are the questions that separate consultants who know their work from those who know how to pitch it:

- **"What's the first thing you'd change about our current marketing, and why?"** — Requires them to have actually looked.
- **"Walk me through an engagement that didn't go as planned. What happened?"** — Failure analysis reveals more than success stories.
- **"How do you measure success in the first 90 days?"** — If the answer is all activity metrics and no outcome metrics, that's a red flag.
- **"Who do you refer clients to when a project is outside your scope?"** — Tests whether they have a real network or just a habit of saying yes.
- **"What would make you walk away from this engagement?"** — Consultants who have standards will have an answer. Those who don't have one will hesitate.

---

## The Bottom Line

A competent **law firm marketing consultant** will make the evaluation process feel like a conversation between peers, not a sales pitch. They'll push back on your assumptions, ask uncomfortable questions about your intake and your budget, and tell you when your expectations need recalibrating. That friction is a good sign.

The ones who agree with everything you say, promise results faster than is realistic, and can't tell you what they *won't* do are the ones who cost the most in the end—not in fees, but in time.
