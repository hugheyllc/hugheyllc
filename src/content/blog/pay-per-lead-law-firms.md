Is pay-per-lead worth it for a law firm? Sometimes, but only as a short-term tool with a hard cost ceiling, not as the foundation of your marketing. Pay-per-lead for law firms means a vendor generates inquiries through its own websites, ads or call centers and sells them to you at a set price per lead. You skip the build time of SEO and the learning curve of paid search. In exchange, you rent demand that belongs to the vendor, and you can lose it the day you stop paying.

The price per lead is the number every vendor shows you, and it is the least important number. What matters is what you pay for each retained client, how many other firms called the same person first, and whether the vendor is operating inside the Bar rules. I have watched firms sign a vendor contract because the per-lead price looked cheap, then discover the leads were shared with three competitors and the real cost per signed case was higher than Google Ads.

This post covers how the economics work, how to compare exclusive and shared leads, what to ask before signing, and the situations where buying leads is a reasonable call.

## How Pay-Per-Lead for Law Firms Works

Lead vendors fall into a few types. Some run their own websites and ads and sell the resulting inquiries. Some operate call centers that screen callers before transferring them. Some are marketplaces where several firms bid on the same inquiry. Google's Local Services Ads also charge by the lead, which I cover in [my guide to Local Services Ads for law firms](/blog/google-local-services-ads-law-firms/).

The commercial model is simple: the vendor owns the traffic and sells you the output. That is the core tradeoff. When you build SEO, a referral network or a strong brand, you own an asset that keeps producing. When you buy leads, your cost stays flat or rises, and you have nothing to show for it when the contract ends.

## Exclusive vs. Shared Leads: What You Are Really Buying

An exclusive lead goes to one firm. A shared lead goes to several, and the person who answers the phone first often wins. Shared leads cost less, but you are racing competitors, and the prospect may already be tired of calls by the time you reach them.

Run the math on your own numbers, not the vendor's. Here is an illustration with made-up figures:

- A shared lead at $40 that your intake team converts at 5 percent costs $800 per signed case.
- An exclusive lead at $120 that converts at 20 percent costs $600 per signed case.

The cheaper lead lost. That is why I push firms to measure [cost per retained client](/blog/cost-per-retained-client-law-firm/) instead of cost per lead. Your conversion rate depends heavily on how fast you call back, so [speed to contact](/blog/law-firm-intake-speed/) matters more with shared leads than anywhere else.

## What Should You Ask a Lead Vendor Before Signing?

Most bad vendor relationships were visible before the contract was signed. Ask these questions and get the answers in writing:

1. Is each lead exclusive, and how many firms receive it? If the answer is vague, assume shared.
2. How are leads generated, and can you see the actual ads, pages and call scripts? Your name and your license may be attached to this material.
3. What counts as a billable lead? Duplicates, wrong practice areas, out-of-state callers and spam should be refundable.
4. What is the return policy, and what is the time limit to claim it? Short windows protect the vendor.
5. Do you get call recordings and the original form data? You need these to audit quality and train intake.
6. Can you cap volume and spend weekly? Open-ended billing is a red flag. My list of [law firm marketing contract red flags](/blog/law-firm-marketing-contract-red-flags/) applies here too.

## Is Buying Legal Leads Allowed Under Bar Rules?

In Florida, you generally cannot give anything of value to someone for recommending your services, but a lawyer may pay the usual charges of a qualifying provider, which includes lead generators. The provider must meet conditions set out in Rule 4-7.22, and the Bar says lawyers must report their participation within 15 days of starting or ending it. Review The Florida Bar's page on [lawyers and qualifying providers](https://www.floridabar.org/ethics/etad/qualifying-providers-lawyer-referral-services/) and the [Handbook on Lawyer Advertising and Solicitation](https://www-media.floridabar.org/uploads/2025/12/Handbook-2025-Approved-by-SCA-12-10-25.pdf) before you sign.

Pay particular attention to a vendor that markets itself in a way that could make a prospect think it is a law firm, and to any arrangement where the vendor takes a share of your fee. Both are the kind of structure the rules target. Other states differ, so check yours, and call the Bar's ethics hotline when a deal looks unusual. I am not a lawyer, and this is not legal advice.

## When Does Buying Leads Make Sense?

Used with discipline, leads can fit a few situations:

- **A new practice area or office** where you have no search presence yet and need volume to train intake and learn what converts.
- **A short-term fill** while SEO and referral work matures, with an agreed end date.
- **A high-value case type** where the allowable cost per signed case is large enough to absorb vendor markup.
- **A firm with a fast, well-trained intake process,** because speed is what turns a purchased lead into a client.

It makes little sense when your intake is slow, when you cannot track outcomes by source, or when leads become your only channel. At that point you have handed control of your pipeline to a vendor. Track every vendor lead through to retention, coding every inquiry by source, and set a cost-per-retained-client ceiling before you spend the first dollar.

## Can You Turn Rented Demand Into Owned Demand?

Yes, and this is where the strategy lives. Treat purchased leads as a source of data. Note which practice areas, locations and questions convert, then build content, local pages and referral relationships around them. Over time you shift budget from rented demand toward demand you own. A vendor lead that never gets a follow-up sequence is wasted, so also review how you handle [leads that go cold after first contact](/blog/law-firm-lead-follow-up-conversion/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much do pay-per-lead services cost for law firms?

Pricing varies widely by practice area, location and whether the lead is exclusive. Personal injury and other competitive categories cost more than lower-value matters. Ignore the quoted price and calculate what you pay per signed and retained case from your own conversion data.

### Are exclusive leads always better than shared leads?

Not always, but they usually convert better because you are not racing other firms. A shared lead can work if your intake responds within minutes and the price is low enough. Compare both on cost per retained client, not cost per lead.

### Is it ethical for a lawyer to pay for leads?

It can be, depending on your state's rules. In Florida, lawyers may pay a qualifying provider's usual charges if the provider meets Rule 4-7.22 conditions, and lawyers must report participation to the Bar. Always confirm with your state bar before signing.

### How do I know if a lead vendor is giving me bad leads?

Audit them. Listen to call recordings, check for duplicates and wrong practice areas, and track every lead through to signed engagement. If the vendor will not share recordings or original form data, treat that as a reason to walk away.

### Should a law firm rely on lead generation companies long term?

I would not. Vendors control the traffic, the price and the rules, and you build no lasting asset. Use leads to fill gaps or test markets, then invest in SEO, referrals and brand so your pipeline does not depend on one supplier.
