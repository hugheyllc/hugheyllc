Why did that consultation not sign? Most firms guess: price, timing, "they went with someone else." **Win-loss analysis for law firms** replaces the guess with an actual answer, gathered directly from the prospect who said no. It means calling a sample of consultations that didn't retain, asking a short set of honest questions, and coding what you hear into patterns your marketing and intake teams can act on.

Sales organizations have run this discipline for decades. Law firms almost never do, because the person who ran the consultation is usually the one who'd have to make the call, and nobody wants to hear "your associate seemed rushed" from a prospect directly. That discomfort is exactly why the data is valuable. Your CRM shows you a status field that says "did not retain." It cannot tell you whether the fee scared them off, whether a competitor called back faster, or whether nobody explained what happens next. This is how you find out, and what to do with the answer once you have it.

## Why Win-Loss Analysis for Law Firms Works Differently Than a CRM Report

A CRM report tells you the outcome. It almost never tells you the cause, because the reason field is filled in by whoever ran the consultation, and people are not reliable narrators of their own losses. An attorney who ran a weak intake call is not going to log "I didn't build rapport" as the loss reason. They'll log "price objection" because it's easier to accept and doesn't require them to change anything.

That gap between the logged reason and the real reason is where win-loss analysis earns its keep. It replaces a self-reported guess with a direct account from the person who actually decided, which is a different category of data entirely. If your firm already tracks [consultation-to-retained conversion rates](/blog/law-firm-marketing-benchmarks/) and the number is lower than it should be, a reason code in the CRM won't tell you what to fix. A conversation with the prospect will.

## Who Should Call the Prospect Who Didn't Hire You?

Not the attorney who ran the consultation, and ideally not anyone on the intake team who was involved in that specific case. Prospects soften their answers when they're talking to the person who might be hurt by the truth. A managing partner, an office manager who wasn't on the original call, or an outside consultant will get a more honest answer than the associate whose performance is actually in question.

Keep the outreach short and low-pressure. A brief call or a two-minute email survey sent within a week or two of the decision works better than a lengthy questionnaire sent a month later, once the details have blurred and the prospect has moved on with whichever firm they chose, or with no firm at all.

Speed matters on your end of this too, and not just for the interview. [Clio's Legal Trends Report](https://www.clio.com/resources/legal-trends/) has repeatedly found that firms responding to a new lead within minutes convert at multiples of the rate of firms that wait even half an hour, which is worth keeping in mind when a prospect tells you follow-up felt slow: it usually was.

## What to Actually Ask a Prospect Who Didn't Sign

Resist the urge to build a long form. Five or six questions, asked conversationally, get better answers than a survey that feels like an audit:

- **Did you hire another firm, or decide not to pursue this at all?** This single question splits your losses into two entirely different problems: competitive losses and no-decision losses.
- **If you hired someone else, what made the difference?** Push past the first answer. "They were cheaper" often means "they explained the fee and we never did."
- **Was there anything about the consultation itself that gave you pause?** This is where you learn about rapport, clarity, and whether the attorney actually addressed the prospect's specific situation.
- **How long did it take us to follow up after the consultation?** Slow follow-up is one of the most common, most fixable loss reasons, and prospects remember exactly how long they waited.
- **Is there anything we could have done differently?** Open-ended, and often where the most useful answer shows up.

## Coding Loss Reasons So the Data Means Something

A stack of anecdotes is not an analysis. Once you've run ten or fifteen of these conversations, code each one into a small set of categories: fee objection, went with a competitor, follow-up too slow, didn't feel understood, timing or need changed, no decision made. Track the counts by practice area and by which attorney or intake staffer ran the consultation.

Patterns show up fast. If "follow-up too slow" dominates your fee-based practice areas, that's an intake staffing problem, not a pricing problem, and no amount of adjusting your rates will fix it. If one attorney's consultations lose disproportionately to "didn't feel understood," that's a coaching conversation, not a marketing one. This is the same discipline behind a broader [intake process audit](/blog/law-firm-intake-process-audit/), narrowed down to the specific moment a prospect chose not to move forward.

Practice management platforms already build reporting around this idea for the client relationships firms do land. [Thomson Reuters documents a "Clients/Prospects Lost" report](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/help/practice-cs/reporting/clients-prospects-lost-report) as a standard part of client management for exactly this reason: firms that only track who they won are missing half the picture. Your loss codes deserve the same structure, whether you're tracking them in that kind of module or a simple spreadsheet your intake manager owns.

## Closing the Loop: Getting Findings Back to Marketing and Intake

Win-loss analysis for law firms only pays off if the findings actually change something. If prospects consistently say the fee structure wasn't clear before the consultation, that's a fix for your website copy and your intake scripts, not a note that sits in a spreadsheet. If they say a competitor called back within the hour and you took two days, that's a staffing or workflow conversation your intake manager needs to have this week.

Run this quarterly, not once. A single round tells you what happened with a handful of prospects. A recurring process tells you whether the fixes you made actually moved the [consultation-to-retained rate](/blog/cost-per-retained-client-law-firm/), and it catches new problems before they become a pattern across dozens of lost cases. Feed the loss codes into whatever system your team already tracks [lead scoring and qualification](/blog/law-firm-lead-scoring-crm-qualification/) in, so the findings sit next to the data your intake team looks at daily instead of a report nobody opens.

If your firm has never asked a single prospect why they didn't sign, that's a gap worth closing before you spend another dollar trying to generate more leads. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is win-loss analysis for a law firm?

It's the practice of interviewing prospects after a consultation that didn't result in a signed client, to learn the real reason they chose another firm or decided not to hire anyone. The findings get coded into patterns that inform marketing messaging, intake training, and follow-up speed.

### How many lost consultations should we interview?

Ten to fifteen per quarter is enough to start seeing patterns without turning this into a full-time project. Prioritize practice areas or attorneys where your consultation-to-retained rate is lowest, since that's where the findings will matter most.

### Who should conduct the win-loss interview?

Someone who wasn't involved in the original consultation, ideally a managing partner, office manager, or outside consultant. Prospects give more honest answers to a neutral party than to the attorney or staffer whose performance is actually being evaluated.

### Isn't this the same as an intake follow-up audit?

No. A follow-up audit looks at your internal process and timestamps. Win-loss analysis goes to the source, the prospect, and asks them directly why they didn't sign, which often surfaces reasons your internal data can't show you.

### How is this different from tracking CRM loss reason codes?

CRM reason codes are self-reported by whoever ran the consultation, and people rarely log their own missteps accurately. A direct interview with the prospect replaces that guess with what actually happened, from the person who made the decision.
