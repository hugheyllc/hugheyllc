Who should elder law marketing speak to? In most cases, the adult child, not the aging parent. The parent is the client or the subject of the matter, but the person typing the search, reading the website and placing the first call is frequently a son or daughter in the middle of a crisis, often living in a different city.

That changes nearly everything about how an elder law firm should market. The adult child is not browsing for a legacy plan. They are trying to answer urgent questions: how a nursing home will be paid for, whether a parent can still sign documents, what happens if a parent cannot manage money anymore. They search late at night, compare several firms, and often discuss the decision with siblings before anyone calls.

Marketing written only for a 78-year-old planning ahead misses that reader entirely. Marketing that treats the adult child as the primary audience, while respecting the parent as the person whose wishes govern, tends to earn the first conversation.

This post covers who actually searches, what they search for, how to build pages and intake around them, and which referral relationships feed the practice.

## Why Adult Children Drive Elder Law Marketing

The [Administration for Community Living](https://acl.gov/) tracks the aging population and the family caregivers who support it, and the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2023/population-estimates-characteristics.html) documents the steady growth of the older population. Demand for elder law services follows that trend, and so does the number of children coordinating care from a distance.

[AARP's caregiving research](https://www.aarp.org/caregiving/) describes how much work family caregivers take on, much of it with no legal guidance. That is the moment a firm becomes visible: a hospital discharge, a dementia diagnosis, a sudden fall, a letter from a facility.

Three traits define this audience:

- **Stressed and time-pressed.** They are balancing a job, their own family and a parent's emergency.
- **Often remote.** Many do not live near the parent, so search results and the website carry more weight than word of mouth in the parent's town.
- **Collaborative.** Siblings, a spouse and sometimes a financial planner weigh in, so the website must be easy to forward and explain.

Estate planning clients overlap with this group, and the [estate planning marketing guide](/blog/estate-planning-marketing-law-firms/) covers the planning side. Elder law is distinguished by urgency and by the presence of a third party making the call.

## What Do Adult Children Search for When a Parent Needs Legal Help?

Their queries are longer and more specific than typical legal searches. They describe a situation instead of naming a service. Examples include how to protect a parent's home from nursing home costs, how Medicaid looks back at gifts, what guardianship involves, and what to do when a parent with dementia has no power of attorney.

Map your content to those situations:

1. **Crisis questions:** nursing home admission, hospital discharge, sudden incapacity.
2. **Cost questions:** Medicaid eligibility, asset protection, paying for long-term care.
3. **Authority questions:** power of attorney, guardianship, what a family member can and cannot do.
4. **Planning questions:** special needs trusts, long-term care planning for a spouse.

Each deserves its own page written in plain language, with a clear next step. A single "Elder Law Services" page listing every term cannot rank for these questions or reassure a worried reader. Depth on narrow questions beats breadth.

## How Should an Elder Law Website Speak to Two Audiences?

Address the adult child directly, while keeping the parent's dignity and autonomy visible. Headlines can acknowledge the situation ("When a parent can no longer manage on their own") without scaring or condescending.

Practical page elements that help:

- A short explanation of who the firm represents, since families are often unsure whether the parent, the child or both are clients.
- Plain-language timelines: what happens first, what documents are needed, how long things usually take.
- Honest cost framing, such as how fees are structured, without promising outcomes.
- Photos and copy that avoid clichés about sad hands and sunsets.

Attorney credentials matter more here because the reader is making a high-stakes decision for someone else. Carefully built [attorney bio pages](/blog/attorney-bio-pages-that-convert/) that show elder law focus, certifications where they exist and a human voice give a reader permission to call.

Advertising claims about elder law credentials and results fall under bar rules. Review the [Florida Bar advertising rules](/blog/florida-bar-law-firm-advertising-rules/) before publishing specialty language, and do not imply guaranteed Medicaid outcomes.

## Which Referral Sources and Channels Reach Families Early?

Search is the main channel for distant children, but referral relationships reach families before they start searching. Hospital discharge planners, social workers, senior living administrators, geriatric care managers and financial planners meet families at the moment of need. A useful pattern is education over solicitation: a short caregiver workshop for a facility's families, or a clear one-page guide a care manager can hand out.

Educational events suit this audience because the audience is already looking for answers. A recorded session on Medicaid basics or guardianship can be reused on the site and in email.

Paid search can capture high-intent queries, but only with landing pages matched to the situation. Sending "nursing home Medicaid" clicks to a generic homepage wastes the budget.

## Intake: Where Most Elder Law Leads Are Lost

An adult child reaching out is usually mid-crisis. Firms that review their call recordings often find that the first person to respond gets the consultation, and that families leave voicemails few firms return promptly. [Speed in intake](/blog/law-firm-intake-speed/) matters here more than in many practice areas.

Intake staff should know how to handle:

- A caller who is not the client and cannot yet say what the parent wants.
- Questions about capacity and who may sign what.
- Requests for a meeting at a hospital, facility or home, or by video for out-of-town children.
- Gentle, clear follow-up through [a structured lead follow-up process](/blog/law-firm-lead-follow-up-conversion/) for families who need days to decide.

Track which situations produce signed clients. Over time, that tells the firm which pages and channels deserve investment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do elder law attorneys get clients?

Elder law firms typically get clients through search results aimed at adult children, referrals from care professionals and financial planners, and educational events. Most firms benefit from combining them, since each reaches families at a different stage of a parent's decline or planning.

### Who is the target audience for elder law marketing?

The primary audience is often an adult child, commonly in midlife, who is coordinating care or finances for a parent. A secondary audience is the older adult planning ahead. Messaging should serve the child's urgency while respecting the parent's wishes and role.

### Is elder law the same as estate planning?

They overlap but differ. Estate planning focuses on documents and asset transfer. Elder law adds long-term care funding, Medicaid planning, guardianship and incapacity issues. Marketing reflects that, with more urgent searches and more third-party decision makers in elder law.

### What should an elder law website include?

An elder law website should include separate pages for specific situations such as Medicaid planning and guardianship, plain-language explanations of process and fees, attorney pages showing elder law focus, and an easy way to call or schedule, including by video for out-of-town family members.
