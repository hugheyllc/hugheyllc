Hiring your first in-house marketing person is one of the most consequential decisions a law firm will make, and getting it wrong is expensive in ways that go far beyond salary. The right hire becomes the engine behind your firm's visibility, reputation, and client pipeline. The wrong hire — someone who looks great on paper but doesn't understand the unique dynamics of legal marketing — can set you back eighteen months or more, leaving partners frustrated and questioning whether marketing works at all. What you're really looking for is someone who combines genuine marketing craft with the patience, diplomacy, and industry literacy to operate inside a law firm environment, which is unlike almost any other professional services setting. Prioritize strategic judgment, strong writing, relationship skills, and enough digital fluency to manage vendors and measure results. Avoid candidates fixated on vanity metrics, quick wins, or a single narrow channel. That combination is rarer than most managing partners expect, and knowing exactly what to screen for before you post the job listing will save you enormous headaches down the road.

## Why Law Firms Are a Unique Marketing Environment

Before you write a job description, you need to accept something important: law firms are not like other businesses, and your marketing hire needs to understand that from day one. Partners have competing priorities, varying levels of buy-in to marketing efforts, and deeply ingrained habits around client relationships. A candidate who thrived at a tech startup or a consumer brand may be completely blindsided by the pace and politics of a law firm partnership.

In my experience, the candidates who succeed long-term are the ones who have either worked inside a firm before or who have worked with law firms as an agency or consulting client. That direct exposure matters more than almost any credential on the resume.

## The Skill Sets That Actually Matter

When I talk to managing partners about this hire, they often lead with the wrong wish list. They want someone who "does social media" or "handles the website." Those are tasks, not skill sets. What you actually need is someone who can think strategically, execute consistently, and communicate clearly with attorneys who are skeptical of marketing.

Here are the core competencies I look for:

**Strategic thinking.** Can this person look at your firm's practice areas, competitive landscape, and referral relationships and develop a coherent plan? Or do they jump straight to tactics?

**Content creation.** Legal marketing lives and dies on content — articles, newsletters, LinkedIn posts, website copy. Your hire needs to be a strong writer or editor, full stop.

**Relationship support skills.** Much of law firm marketing is not advertising. It's helping attorneys nurture referral relationships, prepare for speaking engagements, and follow up after events. Your marketing person needs to be good with people, not just pixels.

**Basic digital fluency.** They don't need to be a developer, but they need to understand SEO fundamentals, email platforms, and analytics dashboards well enough to manage vendors and make smart decisions.

## What to Look For Beyond the Resume

The resume tells you what someone has done. The interview tells you whether they can do it here. I always recommend giving candidates a practical scenario during the interview process — something like: "One of our partners wants to grow her estate planning practice. Walk me through how you'd approach that in the first ninety days."

What you're listening for is process. Do they ask clarifying questions? Do they think about the attorney's existing relationships before reaching for advertising? Do they mention content, referral development, and thought leadership — or do they go straight to paid digital? As I've written about in [Thought Leadership on LinkedIn](/blog/legal-thought-leadership-on-linkedin/), an attorney's personal presence and credibility online is often the highest-ROI marketing activity a firm can invest in. Your marketing hire should already believe that and be able to articulate why.

Also pay attention to how they talk about attorneys. Candidates who express frustration with professionals who "don't get marketing" or "won't cooperate" are waving a red flag. The best law firm marketers I've ever seen treat attorney buy-in as their job to earn, not a problem to complain about.

## The Experience Question: Generalist or Specialist?

For most small and mid-size firms hiring their first in-house marketer, I strongly recommend a generalist over a specialist. A pure social media person won't be able to manage your website refresh. A pure events coordinator won't be able to write your newsletter. In the early stages of building an internal marketing function, you need someone who can do a lot of different things at a competent level.

From what I've seen, firms that hire a narrow specialist first end up frustrated because the hire can't cover the full scope of what marketing actually requires. You'll get more value from someone who is a B+ at eight different things than someone who is an A+ at one.

That said, if your firm already has a specific growth problem — say, you're generating plenty of leads but converting few of them — a specialist might make sense. Just be honest with yourself about what the actual gap is.

## Internal Referrals and Relationship Marketing Knowledge

One area that separates strong candidates from great ones is their understanding of referral-based business development. Most law firm revenue still flows through relationships — other attorneys, accountants, financial advisors, and longtime clients who send business your way. Your marketing hire needs to appreciate that ecosystem and know how to support it.

As I've laid out in [Building an Internal Referral Program](/blog/building-an-internal-referral-program-law-firm/), your own team is often the best source of new business, and a savvy marketing person will know how to systematize those internal conversations so opportunities don't fall through the cracks. Candidates who think only in terms of external campaigns — ads, SEO, social — miss half the picture.

## Red Flags to Watch For

I've seen firms get burned by a few recurring mistakes. Watch out for candidates who talk mostly about followers, likes, and impressions without connecting any of it to client development. Vanity metrics are not the same as business results, and a strong candidate will know the difference.

Also be cautious of anyone who promises quick wins. Legal marketing is a long game. Brand-building, content credibility, and referral relationships take months to develop. A candidate who is selling you fast results either doesn't understand legal marketing or is telling you what you want to hear.

Finally, make sure the candidate actually wants to work inside a law firm, not just needs a job. Motivation matters enormously in this role. The best in-house law firm marketers I've worked with are genuinely curious about the law and see themselves as long-term partners in building the firm's practice — not just someone passing through on their way somewhere else.

## Setting Your New Hire Up for Success

Hiring the right person is only half the equation. Many firms find a strong candidate and then promptly undermine them by failing to give them access to partners, excluding them from business development conversations, or loading them up with administrative tasks that have nothing to do with marketing.

Your new hire needs a clear mandate, a realistic budget, and visible support from firm leadership. Without those three things, even the best marketing professional will struggle to make a dent.
