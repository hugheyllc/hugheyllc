How does mass tort marketing work? A firm pays to reach a large pool of people who may have been harmed by the same drug, device or product, screens those inquiries against strict case criteria, and signs the small share that qualify. The advertising is the visible part. The money is made or lost in the screening, the vendor arrangements and the timing.

Mass tort marketing differs from ordinary personal injury marketing because the claim is shared. Thousands of potential plaintiffs share one defendant and one theory, so many firms chase the same people at the same moment. Prices rise quickly once a litigation gets attention, and the cost of a signed case depends less on the ad and more on how fast and how accurately a firm can tell a real claim from a weak one.

Most of the burned firms share a pattern. They treat the campaign as a lead purchase instead of an operation, hand intake to a vendor they cannot audit, and discover months later that a large share of signed cases will not survive a records review.

## How Does Mass Tort Lead Generation Work?

The model has four moving parts, and each one can fail independently.

- **Demand capture:** paid search, social, television, streaming and direct response ads aimed at people who match the exposure profile.
- **Qualification:** a questionnaire or call script that tests the claim against the criteria the litigation requires, such as product use, diagnosis and dates.
- **Retention:** an e-signature or live sign-up, often followed by a records collection process.
- **Case placement:** the retained claimant is kept in the firm's inventory, co-counseled out, or referred to the firm leading the litigation.

Many cases are consolidated in federal court under [28 U.S.C. 1407](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1407), which allows related civil actions from different districts to be transferred for coordinated pretrial proceedings. That structure is why timing matters. Filing deadlines, bellwether schedules and settlement programs set the window in which a signed claimant has value.

## Where Do Mass Tort Firms Get Burned?

Four failure points account for most of the losses reported by firms that audit their campaigns.

### Weak vetting of the claimant

A lead that says yes to every screening question is not the same as a documented claim. Firms that skip early records checks pay to sign people who cannot prove use, injury or timing. The cost shows up later as dropped cases, and by then the advertising money is gone.

### Duplicate and double-signed claimants

In a crowded litigation, one person can respond to several campaigns and sign with more than one firm. Without a check at sign-up, a firm may pay twice or more for a case it does not hold. A duplicate-check step before retention is cheap compared with the cost of a contested signature.

### Fraudulent or recycled leads

High prices attract bad actors. Bot submissions, coached screening answers and resold lists all inflate lead counts. The same dynamics described in [click fraud for law firms](/blog/click-fraud-law-firms/) apply here, with higher stakes per fake inquiry.

### Chasing a tort after the peak

Spend that made sense in the first months of a litigation can stop making sense once the claimant pool is saturated or a key ruling goes the wrong way. Firms that set no stop-loss rule keep buying cases whose expected value has fallen.

## What Do the Ethics Rules Say About Buying Mass Tort Leads?

This is the area where marketing decisions turn into bar exposure. ABA Model Rule 7.2 generally prohibits giving anything of value to someone for recommending a lawyer, with a narrow exception for the usual charges of a lead generator that meets stated conditions. The [ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/professional_responsibility/publications/model_rules_of_professional_conduct/) also hold lawyers responsible for supervising nonlawyer vendors who act on the firm's behalf.

Florida adds its own requirements. The [Florida Bar rules on lawyer advertising](https://www.floridabar.org/rules/rrtfb/) cover the content of ads, solicitation limits and the treatment of lawyer referral services. A firm running mass tort ads in several states must check each state's rules, because what is permitted in one jurisdiction may not be in another. This is not legal advice, and firms should confirm their approach with ethics counsel before signing a vendor agreement. The related guide to [Florida Bar advertising rules](/blog/florida-bar-law-firm-advertising-rules/) covers the baseline.

## How Should a Firm Evaluate a Mass Tort Lead Vendor?

A vendor relationship is safer when the firm keeps control of the points that matter. Compare the arrangement against this list:

1. The firm sees the actual ad creative and landing pages before they run.
2. The firm receives source data for each lead, not only a summary count.
3. Exclusivity terms are written down, along with the replacement policy for duplicates and disqualified claimants.
4. Payment is tied to a defined event, such as a verified lead or a signed retainer, and the definition is specific.
5. Call recordings and screening records are available for review.

## How Should a Firm Measure Mass Tort Marketing Performance?

Cost per lead is the wrong headline number. The figure that matters is cost per retained case that survives vetting, tracked by source and by week. A [cost per retained client](/blog/cost-per-retained-client-law-firm/) view exposes vendors whose leads look cheap and then collapse in review.

Firms that run these campaigns with discipline also track time from inquiry to first contact, screening pass rate, share of signed cases that clear the records stage, and the spend ceiling at which the campaign stops. Intake speed has an outsized effect when many firms contact the same person, so response time belongs in the same dashboard.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is mass tort marketing?

Mass tort marketing is advertising and lead generation aimed at people harmed by the same product, drug or event. The goal is to find claimants who meet the criteria of a specific litigation, screen them, and sign the qualified ones as clients.

### Is it legal for a law firm to buy mass tort leads?

It can be, but rules vary by state and by how the arrangement is structured. Model Rule 7.2 and state versions limit payments for referrals, and several states regulate lead generators directly. A firm should review its vendor agreement with ethics counsel before buying leads.

### Why do signed mass tort cases fall apart after retention?

Many do not survive records review. Common causes include missing proof of product use, a diagnosis outside the litigation criteria, claims filed past the deadline, or a claimant who signed with another firm first. Early vetting reduces these losses.

### How can a firm avoid fraudulent mass tort leads?

Require source data for each lead, review call recordings, run duplicate checks before retention, and tie vendor payment to a verified event. Sudden spikes in lead volume with falling signed-case rates are a warning sign worth investigating.
