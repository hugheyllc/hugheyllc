Can a law firm use text message marketing? Yes, but only with documented consent and a clear purpose. Text message marketing for law firms works best for responding to people who already contacted the firm: confirming an inquiry, reminding a prospect about a consultation, or following up on a missed call. It works poorly, and carries the most legal risk, as a way to blast promotions to a purchased or old list.

The reason is the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), which restricts automated and marketing texts sent to mobile phones without the recipient's permission. Florida adds its own telephone solicitation statute, with a private right of action that plaintiffs' lawyers actively use. A firm that texts without proof of consent can end up as a defendant instead of a service provider.

Used inside intake, texting is fast, personal and hard to ignore. Used as a broadcast channel, it creates exposure that rarely pays back. This guide covers where texting belongs, what consent has to look like, how opt-outs must work, and how to measure whether it is helping.

## Where Text Message Marketing for Law Firms Belongs in Intake

The strongest use case is speed. A prospect who filled out a form or called after hours is comparing firms, and the first firm to respond usually has the advantage. [Response speed in law firm intake](/blog/law-firm-intake-speed/) is one of the most controllable factors in conversion, and a text can acknowledge an inquiry within seconds while a person prepares to call.

Safe, high-value intake uses include:

- An immediate acknowledgment after a web form or missed call, naming the firm and stating when someone will respond.
- Consultation reminders and confirmations, which cut no-shows without adding sales pressure.
- Requests for missing information, such as a document or a better time to talk.
- A short follow-up when a prospect stopped replying, as part of a broader [lead follow-up process](/blog/law-firm-lead-follow-up-conversion/).

Each of these responds to an action the person took. That context matters both for compliance and for how the message lands. A reminder about a booked consultation feels like service. An unsolicited promotion feels like spam.

## What Does TCPA Compliance Require for Law Firm Texts?

TCPA compliance for law firms starts with consent. Marketing texts sent with automated technology generally require the recipient's prior express written consent, which means a clear agreement, signed or electronically accepted, that the person is willing to receive them. Informational texts tied to a request the person made are treated differently, but the safest practice is to collect texting consent at the same moment the person gives a phone number.

A compliant consent process has five features:

1. A separate, unchecked checkbox on web forms, not buried in a privacy policy or pre-selected.
2. Plain language stating that the firm will send texts, that message frequency varies, and that consent is not a condition of hiring the firm.
3. A timestamped record of the consent, the phone number and the form version.
4. Consent that matches the use. Permission to text about a consultation is not permission to enroll someone in a monthly promotion.
5. Retention of those records long enough to defend a complaint.

Rules and court interpretations in this area change, and several sources conflict on current details. Firms should have a telecom or consumer protection attorney review the consent language and the texting platform's configuration. This article is not legal advice.

## Florida Adds a Second Layer of Risk

Florida firms face the state's [telephone solicitation statute](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2023/501.059), which covers text messages sent to solicit a sale. It requires prior express written consent for automated solicitation and gives a called party the right to sue. Recoverable damages are the greater of actual damages or $500 per violation, and a willful violation can be tripled. The prevailing party recovers attorney's fees.

For text solicitations, the statute gives a recipient who replies STOP a path to sue only after the sender has had 15 days to stop. That grace period is narrow, and it does not help a firm that never built a reliable opt-out system.

Texts are also attorney advertising in many contexts. The [Florida Bar advertising rules](/blog/florida-bar-law-firm-advertising-rules/) apply to the content, and direct solicitation limits are strict. Review the current rules on [The Florida Bar's rules page](https://www.floridabar.org/rules/rrtfb/) before sending anything that reads as outreach to someone who has not contacted the firm.

## How Should Opt-Outs and Message Content Work?

Opt-out handling is where firms most often fail. A texting program should honor any reasonable opt-out language, not only the word STOP. Replies such as "stop texting," "unsubscribe" or "take this number off the list" should end messaging, and the opt-out must reach every system that can text that number, including the CRM, the call tracking platform and any reminder tool.

Practical standards for message content:

- Identify the firm in the first message.
- Keep texts short, specific and tied to the person's request.
- Avoid case details, results or anything confidential. Texts can be seen by anyone holding the phone.
- Send within reasonable daytime hours, typically morning to early evening in the recipient's time zone.
- Include opt-out instructions in marketing messages.

Email follows a different law. The FTC's [CAN-SPAM compliance guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business) covers commercial email, including opt-out requirements, so a firm's email program and text program need separate consent and suppression lists. For the email side, see this guide to [law firm email marketing](/blog/law-firm-email-marketing/).

## How to Measure Whether Texting Is Working

Texting should be judged by intake outcomes, not by send volume. Firms that track results carefully compare a few numbers before and after launch:

- Percentage of inquiries reached within five minutes.
- Consultation show rate for prospects who received reminders.
- Reply rate to first follow-up texts.
- Opt-out and complaint rate, which signals whether the content or consent process needs work.

Tag every text-sourced consultation in the CRM so the results are visible beside calls and forms. A common pattern in audits of intake workflows is that texting improves contact rates but nobody tracks it, so the firm cannot tell whether it is worth the platform cost. An [abandoned lead audit](/blog/abandoned-lead-audit-law-firms/) is a useful way to find out how many inquiries currently go unanswered, which shows how much room texting has to help.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can lawyers text potential clients?

Lawyers can text people who contacted the firm and gave consent, such as confirming an inquiry or a consultation. Unsolicited texts to people who have not requested contact raise consent problems under the TCPA, Florida law and state bar solicitation rules, and should be avoided without legal review.

### Do law firms need written consent to send text messages?

For marketing texts sent with automated systems, written consent is generally required, and an electronic checkbox with clear disclosure can satisfy that. Firms should keep timestamped records. Because rules and court decisions evolve, an attorney should review the consent language before launch.

### What happens if a law firm violates the TCPA?

Violations can lead to private lawsuits, including class actions, with statutory damages per message under federal law. Florida's statute adds its own claims and fee-shifting. The practical risk is highest for firms texting purchased lists or ignoring opt-out requests.

### How should a law firm handle text opt-outs?

Honor any reasonable opt-out request immediately, confirm it once, and suppress the number across every system that can send texts. Test the process regularly, because a CRM, a reminder tool and a call platform can each keep sending after the others have stopped.
