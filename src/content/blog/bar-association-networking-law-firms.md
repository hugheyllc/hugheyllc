Does bar association networking actually bring in clients? It can, but not through membership alone. Firms that get matters from bar involvement treat it as a relationship program: a small number of committees, steady participation over years, and a system for following up with the lawyers who could realistically send work.

Most of the value is indirect. Other lawyers refer matters they cannot take because of a conflict, a full docket or a different practice area. They send those matters to people they have seen work, not to people whose name appears on a dues list. That is why [a referral program only works when it is tracked](/blog/law-firm-referral-tracking/) and why attendance at one mixer rarely changes anything.

The skeptics have a point. The people in the room are mostly other lawyers, and a bar event is a poor place to meet a consumer who needs a divorce attorney. The upside is narrower and more durable: referral partners, co-counsel, local counsel work and a reputation that makes everything else in your marketing easier to believe.

This post covers what to join, how to show up, how to follow up and how to measure whether the time is paying back.

## Why Bar Association Networking Works (and Why It Often Does Not)

The pattern is consistent across the commentary from practicing lawyers. Passive membership produces almost nothing. Sustained, visible contribution produces referrals, usually after a year or more.

The California Lawyers Association's solo and small firm section [advises lawyers to join at least two committees](https://calawyers.org/solo-small-firm/musings-on-bar-engagement-to-build-your-practice/), one tied to the practice area and one on a different interest, and notes that a leadership role is not required but passive membership is not enough. That matches what firms see in practice: the lawyers who get referred are the ones colleagues have worked beside on a project.

Three things separate productive bar networking from expensive dues:

- **Repeated contact.** Seeing the same lawyers at monthly committee meetings builds familiarity that a single annual event cannot.
- **Visible competence.** Presenting a CLE, drafting a section newsletter piece or running a subcommittee shows how you think, which is what a referring lawyer needs to know.
- **A clear practice identity.** Lawyers cannot refer to someone whose focus is vague. A one-sentence description of the matters you want is more useful than a business card.

## Which Bar Associations and Committees Should a Firm Join?

Start with a short list rather than a long one. Most firms get better returns from two or three carefully chosen groups than from six memberships they rarely use.

### Match the group to who sends work

Referral sources differ by practice area. An estate planning firm benefits from the real property and elder law sections. A personal injury firm often gets more from a trial lawyers association than from the general county bar. Reviewing [which professionals send each practice area the most consistent leads](/blog/2026-07-26-industry-specific-referral-sources-who-sends-your-practice-a/) helps decide where to spend limited hours.

### Choose one adjacent committee

Lawyers in complementary fields refer to each other because the matters rarely compete. A family law attorney who sits on a business law committee meets the people whose clients are about to divorce. A defense firm that joins a probate section meets the lawyers who need litigation counsel.

### Count the cost honestly

Dues are the smallest cost. The real expense is attorney hours. Firms that decide in advance how many hours per month they will invest, and who will attend, avoid the common outcome of paying for memberships nobody uses.

## How to Build Referral Relationships After the Meeting

The meeting is the start. Most of the return comes from what happens in the 30 days after it.

1. **Follow up within a week.** A short note referencing something specific from the conversation works better than a generic "great to meet you."
2. **Move to a one-on-one.** A coffee or lunch with a lawyer in a complementary practice is where referral relationships actually form.
3. **Refer first when it is appropriate.** Lawyers tend to send work to colleagues they have sent work to, so a genuine, unforced referral often starts the exchange.
4. **Keep contact light and regular.** A quarterly check-in or a useful article beats a sales pitch.
5. **Connect online.** A [LinkedIn connection strategy](/blog/2026-08-29-linkedin-networking-for-business-development-turning-connect/) keeps the relationship visible between meetings.

Your online presence also matters here. A lawyer who is considering a referral will often look up the recipient first, which makes [attorney bio pages that convert](/blog/attorney-bio-pages-that-convert/) and a site that supports referral partners part of the networking program. Hughey LLC sees firms lose referrals simply because the referred lawyer's website did not make the practice focus clear.

## What Do the Ethics Rules Say About Networking and Referrals?

Rules vary by state, and this is not legal advice. The general principle is that a lawyer should not give anything of value for a recommendation or require referrals as a condition of membership.

The Florida Bar reported that an ethics committee found a lawyer may join a business networking organization [as long as the lawyer does not personally solicit cases or trade referrals as a quid pro quo](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-news/lawyers-may-join-business-networking-organizations/). New York reached a stricter result: [NYSBA Ethics Opinion 791](https://nysba.org/ethics-opinion-791/) concluded that a lawyer may not join a networking organization that requires referrals in exchange for legal business.

The practical takeaway is to check your own jurisdiction's rules before joining any group with required referral quotas or fees tied to referrals, and to keep documentation of how any referral arrangement works.

## How to Measure Whether Bar Networking Is Paying Back

Bar networking feels productive, which is exactly why it needs measurement. Track a short list:

- Hours spent per quarter, by attorney
- Referrals received, with the source lawyer and the matter type
- Matters signed and the fees collected from those referrals
- Referrals sent out, since reciprocity drives the relationship

Most intake systems do not capture referring lawyers reliably, so add a required "referred by" field and review it monthly. The same discipline that applies to tracking referral sources generally applies here. After 12 to 18 months, the data shows which groups deserve more time and which should be dropped.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does joining a bar association help a law firm get clients?

Membership alone rarely does. Referrals tend to come from committee work, speaking and repeated contact with lawyers in related practice areas, usually after a year or more. Consumers seldom find a lawyer through the bar, so the value is mostly lawyer-to-lawyer referrals and reputation.

### How many bar associations should a lawyer join?

Two or three is a practical range for most firms. Choose one tied to the practice area, one adjacent field whose lawyers meet your likely clients, and possibly a local group for relationships. More memberships usually dilute the time available for any of them.

### How do you get referrals from other lawyers?

Be specific about the matters you want, take part in committee work where others see your competence, meet colleagues one on one, and refer matters when appropriate. Lawyers refer to people they know and trust, so consistency matters more than any single event.

### Can lawyers pay for referrals from networking groups?

Generally no. Most states restrict giving anything of value for a recommendation, and some limit groups that require referrals. Rules differ by jurisdiction, so check your state bar's ethics opinions before joining any referral-based group. This is not legal advice.
