Not all practice areas are created equal when it comes to profitability. In my experience working with law firms across the country, the services partners assume are their biggest earners often turn out to be the ones quietly dragging down margins. Understanding where your firm actually makes money — not just where it generates revenue — is one of the most important strategic exercises a managing partner can undertake.

## Revenue vs. Profit: Why the Distinction Matters

Many firms conflate top-line revenue with profitability. A practice area can bill significant fees while consuming disproportionate overhead, partner time, and support resources.

True profitability accounts for direct costs like associate hours, paralegal support, and court fees, but also the indirect costs that are harder to trace — malpractice insurance allocations, administrative burden, client acquisition costs, and the opportunity cost of partner attention. Until you're measuring both sides of the equation, you're flying blind.

## The Most Common Blind Spots I See

The practice areas that generate the most client inquiries are often the least profitable. High-volume, lower-fee work tends to look attractive on a revenue dashboard but falls apart when you run the numbers on time investment.

Contingency-based practices are a good example. Personal injury and mass tort work can produce large settlements, but the cash flow gaps, case costs carried over months or years, and write-off rates at resolution can erode margins significantly. Many firms underestimate just how capital-intensive this work is until they stress-test their cash position.

On the transactional side, residential real estate closings are another common trap. The fees are modest, the volume required to make it meaningful is high, and the administrative infrastructure needed to process that volume adds overhead quickly. In my experience, this is one of the first practice areas that smart firm administrators look at when they're trying to improve profitability.

## Practice Areas That Tend to Perform Well

While every firm's situation is different, certain practice types tend to show stronger profitability when analyzed carefully.

**Business transactional work** — M&A, entity formation, and commercial contracts — typically carries strong margins because the work is billed at premium rates, leverage is manageable, and the matters are often self-contained. When you have partners working alongside one or two associates on a well-scoped transaction, the economics are usually favorable.

**Estate planning** done at volume with well-designed systems can be highly profitable. The key word is systems. Firms that have templatized their documents, created efficient client intake workflows, and priced their services clearly tend to make estate planning work extremely well. Firms that handle it ad hoc with significant customization on every matter often struggle to make the numbers work.

**Employment defense for businesses** is another practice area that tends to hold up well on profitability analysis. Hourly rates are strong, the work is recurring, and institutional clients that face ongoing employment litigation tend to value consistency and relationship over price. That dynamic supports billing realization.

## How to Actually Measure Practice Area Profitability

If you don't have a formal system for this analysis, here's a practical starting framework.

Start by pulling fee revenue by practice area over the trailing twelve months. Most practice management systems can generate this. Then allocate direct costs — specifically the time value of all timekeepers who worked on matters in that practice area, based on their fully-loaded cost (salary, benefits, taxes, and a proportional share of direct overhead like office space).

The gap between fee revenue and direct costs gives you a gross contribution margin by practice group. This alone will often surprise you.

The next step is harder but important: allocate shared overhead. Marketing spend, administrative staff time, technology costs, and insurance should all be distributed across practice areas in some reasonable proportion — whether by revenue, headcount, or matter volume. There's no single right method, but you have to do it. Practices that require heavy marketing investment or disproportionate administrative support need to carry that cost.

Many firms I work with have never completed this exercise at a detailed level. When they do, they typically find one or two practice areas that are significantly underperforming expectations — and one or two that are quietly carrying the rest of the firm.

## The Partner Time Problem

One of the most underanalyzed costs in any practice area is how it consumes partner time. Partners are the firm's highest-cost resource, and in many small to midsize firms, they're also the primary business development engine.

A practice area that requires heavy partner involvement at every stage — because the work can't be delegated, because clients demand partner attention, or because the complexity doesn't lend itself to leverage — has a structurally limited profitability ceiling. Even if the rates are strong, you can only bill so many partner hours.

Practices with good leverage — where partners can supervise and review while associates do substantial portions of the substantive work — tend to show better profitability at scale. This is one reason why litigation can work well or poorly depending entirely on how the firm is structured and staffed.

## What to Do With This Information

Once you have a clear picture of profitability by practice area, you have real strategic options. You can invest more in the profitable practice areas — adding staff, building systems, and marketing more aggressively. You can look at underperforming areas and ask whether operational changes could improve the economics, or whether pricing needs to be adjusted. And you can have an honest conversation about whether certain practice areas belong in your firm at all.

Some practices exist because a founding partner built them and they've always been there. Some exist because a major client needed the service and the firm accommodated them years ago. Neither of those is a good reason to continue operating at a loss.

In my experience, the firms that grow intentionally are the ones that know their numbers at this level of detail. They make resource decisions based on what the data shows, not what feels true or what partners have always believed. That discipline is what separates a firm that grows profitably from one that just gets bigger.

## Getting Started

You don't need sophisticated financial software to begin this analysis, though good practice management and accounting tools make it significantly easier. Start with what you have, even if the allocations are rough.

A directionally correct profitability picture built from imperfect data is far more useful than no picture at all. Refine the methodology over time, but don't wait for perfect information before you start paying attention to which parts of your firm are actually building wealth.

If you want to understand where your firm should be investing and where it should be pulling back, start with a serious look at profitability by practice area — it's often the most valuable strategic conversation a firm can have.
